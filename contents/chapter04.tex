\chapter{Proposed Method: Adaptive State-Space Design for Real-Time NVC Rate Control}

\section{System Overview}

In this study, we treat the Neural Video Compressor (NVC) system, specifically DCVC-RT, as a non-linear time-variant system. To facilitate the derivation of the rate control algorithm, the encoder is abstracted as a Single-Input Single-Output (SISO) black-box model. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:system_overview}, the system operates as a typical closed-loop control system.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{imgs/system.pdf}
    \caption{Overall architecture of the proposed rate control system. The DCVC-RT is treated as a black-box model. The controller determines the $QP_{final}$ based on the target bitrate and updates its internal model using the feedback loop after encoding.}
    \label{fig:system_overview}
\end{figure}

Let $t$ denote the index of the video frame and $X_t$ be the input raw image frame. For each frame, the objective of the Rate Control System is to determine a final Scaling Parameter ($QP_{final}$) based on the input target bitrate $R_{target}$, such that the actual number of bits generated by the encoder, $R_{real}$, is as close as possible to the budget. The input-output relationship of the system can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    R_{real, t}, \hat{X}_t = \mathcal{F}_{enc}(X_t, QP_{final, t} \mid \Theta_{net})
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}_{enc}(\cdot)$ represents the encoding process of DCVC-RT, $\Theta_{net}$ denotes the fixed, pre-trained weights of the neural network, and $\hat{X}_t$ is the reconstructed image. Since we cannot modify $\Theta_{net}$ in real-time during the encoding process, $QP$ becomes the sole control variable.

As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:system_overview}, our rate control system comprises three cascaded modules:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Rate Allocator:} Responsible for \textbf{Budgeting} at both the Group of Pictures (GOP) and Frame levels. It translates the global bitrate target $R_{target}$ into a specific bit budget for the current frame, denoted as $Bits_{target}$.
    
    \item \textbf{Rate Estimator:} Maintains an internal \textbf{R-QP Model} to describe the relationship between quantization and bitrate. It takes the allocated $Bits_{target}$ as input and predicts the baseline quantization parameter, $QP_{raw}$. This module employs a recursive update mechanism to adapt to video content changes.
    
    \item \textbf{Post-processing:} Ensures the validity and stability of the control signal. It applies \textbf{Clipping} and \textbf{Smoothing} to the raw prediction $QP_{raw}$ to generate the final control command $QP_{final}$, preventing buffer overflow and temporal flickering.
\end{enumerate}

After the encoding of frame $t$ is completed, the system utilizes a \textbf{Feedback Loop} to feed the actual generated bits $R_{real}$ back to the controller. This feedback triggers the \textbf{Update} of the budget status in the Rate Allocator and the \textbf{Parameter Update} in the Rate Estimator, thereby optimizing the decision-making for frame $t+1$.

To facilitate a systematic exposition of the proposed framework, the remainder of this chapter is organized corresponding to the key modules illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:system_overview}. 
First, \textbf{Sections~\ref{sec:rq_modeling} and~\ref{sec:rls_estimation}} detail the core mechanism of the \textbf{Rate Estimator}, covering the derivation of the Log-linear R-Q model and the adaptive Dual-State RLS algorithm. 
Subsequently, \textbf{Section~\ref{sec:bit_allocation}} elaborates on the \textbf{Rate Allocator}, focusing on the sliding window budgeting and hierarchical weight distribution. 
Finally, \textbf{Section~\ref{sec:control_logic}} addresses the \textbf{Post-processing} and control logic, specifically the dual-smoothing mechanisms designed to ensure system stability and safety.


\section{R-Q Modeling and Empirical Analysis}
\label{sec:rq_modeling}

\subsection*{Empirical R-Q Analysis via Exhaustive Search}
To achieve precise rate control in a closed-loop system, the primary task is to establish a mathematical model between the Quantization Parameter (QP) and bits per pixel ($R_{bpp}$). Unlike traditional video coding standards which often model in the $\lambda$-domain, we aim to identify the characteristic that best represents the essence of the DCVC-RT architecture. To eliminate interference from prediction errors and obtain the ground truth relationship, we employed an \textbf{Exhaustive Search} strategy.

During the encoding of test sequences, we forced the encoder to traverse all allowed integer QP values ($QP \in [0, 63]$), thereby obtaining the Ground Truth R-Q Curve for each frame globally. Based on these high-density data points, we evaluated four common candidate models:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Linear Model: $QP = a \cdot R_{bpp} + b$
    \item Power Model: $QP = a \cdot R_{bpp}^b$ (equivalent to $R = \alpha \cdot QP^\beta$~\cite{52})
    \item Exponential Model: $QP = a \cdot e^{b \cdot R_{bpp}}$
    \item Logarithmic Model: $QP = a \cdot \ln(R_{bpp}) + b$
\end{itemize}

Fig.~\ref{fig:rq_curve_fitting} presents the curve fitting results using the exhaustive data. Experimental results show that the \textbf{Logarithmic Model} exhibits the highest goodness-of-fit ($R^2$ score) across test frames. The Linear model tends to underestimate QP in the low bitrate range, while the Power model deviates significantly in the high bitrate range. In contrast, the Log model accurately captures the characteristic of DCVC-RT where \textbf{"bitrate increases exponentially as QP increases."}

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{imgs/qp_r_model.pdf}
    \caption{Empirical R-Q curve fitting based on exhaustive QP search data. The blue curve (Logarithmic model) demonstrates the highest fidelity to the actual data points across the entire bitrate range compared to other candidates.}
    \label{fig:rq_curve_fitting}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Parameter Stability Analysis}
Beyond static fitting accuracy, the \textit{temporal stability} of model parameters is crucial for the design of a stable control algorithm. If model parameters fluctuate violently between adjacent frames, the controller may suffer from overshoot or instability.

As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:param_evolution}, we compared the parameter evolution of the Power Model versus our Logarithmic Model over 600 frames. We selected two representative sequences with distinct characteristics: \textit{Beauty} (Low SI/TI) and \textit{ReadySteadyGo} (High SI/TI).

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{imgs/qp_model_comp.pdf}
    \caption{Comparison of parameter evolution between Power Model (Left) and Log Model (Right). Top row: \textit{Beauty} (Low SI/TI); Bottom row: \textit{ReadySteadyGo} (High TI). The Log Model parameters ($\alpha$) exhibit stable periodicity within a predictable range ($10 \sim 20$), whereas the Power Model parameters fluctuate wildly ($100 \sim 550$) and lack clear patterns.}
    \label{fig:param_evolution}
\end{figure*}

The comparison reveals significant differences in parameter dynamics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Power Model (Left):} The multiplier parameter $a$ exhibits extreme variance and unpredictability. For \textit{Beauty}, $\alpha$ fluctuates between 220 and 530; for \textit{ReadySteadyGo}, it drifts between 120 and 240. Such a wide and erratic dynamic range places a heavy tracking burden on the online estimator, making it prone to divergence.
    \item \textbf{Log Model (Right):} In contrast, our model's slope parameter $\alpha$ (blue line) demonstrates remarkable stability. Regardless of sequence complexity, $\alpha$ remains consistently within the range of $[10, 20]$. It exhibits a stable baseline with regular spikes corresponding to the periodic Refresh Frames, displaying a clear stationary pattern.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Model Linearization and Objective Function}
Based on the empirical evidence and stability analysis above, we officially adopt the Log-linear R-Q Model:
\begin{equation}
    QP_t = \alpha_t \cdot \ln(R_{bpp, t}) + \beta_t
\end{equation}
This finding distinguishes our work from recent NVC rate control research which often utilizes Power Models (requiring non-linear optimization). Our model possesses a critical mathematical advantage: \textbf{Parameter Linearization}.

The equation can be viewed as a standard linear equation $y_t = \mathbf{\theta}_t^T \mathbf{\phi}_t$, where the target variable $y_t$, parameter vector $\mathbf{\theta}_t$, and feature vector $\mathbf{\phi}_t$ are defined as:
\begin{equation}
    y_t = QP_t, \quad \mathbf{\theta}_t = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha_t \\ \beta_t \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{\phi}_t = \begin{bmatrix} \ln(R_{bpp, t}) \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

We define the rate control problem as an optimization problem minimizing the squared prediction error. The instantaneous loss function is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_t(\mathbf{\theta}_t) = \left( QP_{real, t} - \mathbf{\theta}_t^T \mathbf{\phi}_t \right)^2
\end{equation}
This linear structure allows us to avoid complex non-linear solvers and instead employ efficient recursive algorithms for online estimation.

\section{Adaptive Parameter Estimation}
\label{sec:rls_estimation}

\subsection{Recursive Least Squares (RLS) Algorithm}
Due to the non-stationarity of video content, scene changes or fluctuations in complexity cause the optimal model parameters $\alpha_t$ and $\beta_t$ to vary over time. To adapt to this time-variant characteristic, we leverage the \textbf{Recursive Least Squares (RLS)} algorithm.

The RLS algorithm is particularly suitable here because, as established in the previous section, our system model is linear in its parameters. RLS minimizes the cumulative weighted error:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{\theta}}_t = \mathop{\arg\min}_{\mathbf{\theta}} \sum_{i=0}^{t} \lambda^{t-i} \mathcal{L}_i(\mathbf{\theta})
\end{equation}
where $\lambda \in (0, 1]$ is the forgetting factor. This factor balances the importance of historical data against current data, enabling the model to adapt quickly to scene changes.

Fig.~\ref{fig:rls_mechanism} illustrates the update mechanism. The specific steps for each frame $t$ are detailed as follows:

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{imgs/RLS.pdf}
    \caption{Internal mechanism of the RLS Estimator. The model updates its parameter state $\mathbf{\theta}$ and covariance matrix $\mathbf{P}$ recursively based on the prediction error of the previous frame.}
    \label{fig:rls_mechanism}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Calculate Gain Vector:}
    Based on the previous covariance matrix $\mathbf{P}_{t-1}$ and current feature vector $\mathbf{\phi}_t$, compute the Kalman Gain $\mathbf{k}_t$. This gain determines how much the current prediction error should influence the parameter update:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{k}_t = \frac{\mathbf{P}_{t-1} \mathbf{\phi}_t}{\lambda + \mathbf{\phi}_t^T \mathbf{P}_{t-1} \mathbf{\phi}_t}
    \end{equation}
    In our implementation, we set $\lambda = 0.995$ for general P-frames to maintain stability while allowing adaptation.

    \item \textbf{Calculate Prediction Error (Priori Error):}
    Compute the error $e_t$ between the actually observed $QP_{used}$ and the value predicted by the previous state:
    \begin{equation}
        e_t = QP_{used, t} - \mathbf{\phi}_t^T \mathbf{\theta}_{t-1}
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{Update Model Parameters:}
    Correct the parameter estimate $\mathbf{\theta}_t$ using the calculated gain and error:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{\theta}_t = \mathbf{\theta}_{t-1} + \mathbf{k}_t e_t
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{Update Covariance Matrix:}
    Update the inverse correlation matrix $\mathbf{P}$ for the next iteration to reflect the reduced uncertainty:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{P}_t = \frac{1}{\lambda} (\mathbf{P}_{t-1} - \mathbf{k}_t \mathbf{\phi}_t^T \mathbf{P}_{t-1})
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

Through this recursive process, we obtain the optimal estimate $\mathbf{\theta}_t = [\alpha_t, \beta_t]$ immediately after each frame is encoded.

\subsection{Comparison with Gradient-based Methods}

While gradient-based methods such as Least Mean Squares (LMS) or Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) are commonly used for adaptive filtering, we selected RLS for the DCVC-RT rate control system due to its superior convergence properties in non-stationary environments. 

As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:rls_vs_sgd}, although gradient-based methods offer extremely low computational complexity, they rely solely on the instantaneous error gradient. This often results in slow convergence and high sensitivity to the learning rate (step-size). In contrast, RLS utilizes \textbf{second-order statistics} via the covariance matrix $\mathbf{P}$. This allows it to "whiten" the input data, leading to extremely fast convergence (typically within 1-2 frames in our experiments).

Although RLS theoretically has a higher computational cost ($O(N^2)$) compared to LMS ($O(N)$), our model dimension is small ($N=2$, corresponding to parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$). Therefore, the overhead is negligible, making RLS the optimal choice for our specific application where stability and fast tracking are paramount.

\subsection{Challenge: Vulnerability to Refresh Frames}

While RLS provides fast convergence, it possesses a critical limitation when applied to the DCVC-RT architecture: \textbf{Vulnerability to Systemic Outliers}. To understand this, we must first analyze the "Refresh Mechanism" inherent to the codec.

\noindent \textbf{Mechanism of Periodic Refresh}
To mitigate error propagation in the long prediction chain, DCVC-RT employs a periodic feature refresh strategy. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:refresh_mechanism}, the temporal context generation module ($f_{Tcontext}$) contains a \textbf{Refresh Control} switch that alternates between two distinct input paths:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Standard Path (Yellow):} In normal P-frames, the temporal context is derived from the propagated high-dimensional latent feature $F_{t-1}$. This path contains rich semantic history but accumulates quantization errors over time.
    \item \textbf{Refresh Path (Green):} When the refresh counter triggers (e.g., every 32 frames), the switch disconnects the feature propagation. Instead, it forces the model to extract features solely from the reconstructed pixel domain image $\hat{x}_{t-1}$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{imgs/refresh.png}
    \caption{The internal structure of the temporal context module ($f_{Tcontext}$). The \textbf{Refresh Control} determines whether the context is generated from the propagated feature $F_{t-1}$ (Standard Mode) or the reconstructed image $\hat{x}_{t-1}$ (Refresh Mode). This path switching fundamentally alters the signal characteristics.}
    \label{fig:refresh_mechanism}
\end{figure}

Since $\hat{x}_{t-1}$ (Pixel Domain) carries significantly different information density and statistical properties compared to $F_{t-1}$ (Latent Feature Domain), the R-Q relationship undergoes an abrupt structural change during these frames.

\noindent \textbf{The Phenomenon of "Model Shattering"}
From the perspective of a single-state RLS estimator, this structural change acts as a massive outlier. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:model_shattering}, applying a standard RLS filter results in a phenomenon we term \textbf{"Model Shattering."} The process occurs as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Outlier Impact:} When the Refresh Path is activated, the model generates a massive instantaneous prediction error ($e_t$) because the previous parameters $\mathbf{\theta}_{t-1}$ were learned from the Standard Path statistics.
    \item \textbf{Over-Reaction:} Since RLS attempts to minimize the squared error $\sum e_t^2$, it drastically adjusts the parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ to accommodate this single outlier.
    \item \textbf{Covariance Corruption:} The internal covariance matrix $\mathbf{P}_t$, which represents the confidence of the estimator, is corrupted by the non-representative feature vector of the refresh frame.
    \item \textbf{Memory Effect:} Due to the recursive nature, this corrupted state propagates to subsequent frames. The estimator suffers from a "hangover" effect, leading to slow recovery and instability in the following standard P-frames.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[!h] 
    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{imgs/model_shattering.png} 
    \caption{The "Model Shattering" phenomenon caused by single-state RLS. Due to the massive outliers introduced by Refresh Frames, the estimator's covariance matrix is irreversibly corrupted. This causes the model parameters (Alpha and Beta) to collapse towards zero and fail to recover, losing their ability to track the true R-Q characteristic of standard frames.} \label{fig:model_shattering} 
\end{figure}

\subsection{Proposed Solution: Dual-State RLS}

To address the model shattering caused by refresh frames, we propose a \textbf{Dual-State RLS} strategy. The core idea is to prevent "Parameter Poisoning" by isolating the R-D modeling into two distinct states based on frame characteristics.

We define two independent RLS estimators:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{State A (Inter-State):} Updates only on standard P-frames (high temporal dependency).
    \item \textbf{State B (Refresh-State):} Updates only on Refresh frames (low temporal dependency).
\end{itemize}

By temporally decoupling the states, the covariance matrix of the Inter-State remains pristine and unaffected by the statistics of Refresh frames. 

\textbf{Experimental Results:}
Fig.~\ref{fig:dual_state_result} demonstrates the effectiveness of this approach. In the single-state baseline (from the shattering example), parameters would often collapse or exhibit high variance ($\sigma \approx 20.7$). With the Dual-State strategy, the parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ no longer collapse. Instead, they exhibit \textbf{stable oscillations} within physically meaningful bounds. The standard deviation of the parameter $\alpha$ is significantly reduced, indicating a robust estimation process that generalizes well across the entire video sequence.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{imgs/dual_state_stability.png}
    \caption{Performance of the Dual-State RLS. By decoupling the refresh frames, the parameters maintain stability without collapsing to zero. The curves show stable oscillations, confirming that the estimator correctly distinguishes between the two encoding modes.}
    \label{fig:dual_state_result}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Parameter Stabilization (First-Stage Smoothing)}
While the Dual-State RLS responds effectively to system changes, the raw estimated parameters $\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}_t$ may still exhibit high-frequency jitter due to inherent measurement noise in the neural network's feedback. To further stabilize the controller, we implement a \textbf{Parameter Smoothing} layer.

Before the parameters are used for prediction in the next frame, we apply an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) to the RLS output:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{\theta}_{ema, t} = \eta \cdot \mathbf{\theta}_{ema, t-1} + (1 - \eta) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{\theta}}_{t}
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ is the smoothing factor (typically set to 0.7) and $\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}_{t}$ is the instantaneous estimate from the RLS update. The calculation of the base quantization parameter $QP_{raw}$ is then performed using these smoothed parameters $\mathbf{\theta}_{ema}$. This creates a "flywheel effect," preventing the controller from reacting too aggressively to single-frame noise while maintaining the general trend tracking capability of RLS.

\section{Bitrate Allocation Strategy}
\label{sec:bit_allocation}

\subsection{GOP-based Allocation with Sliding Window}
To ensure the stability of the output bitrate while accommodating the specific characteristics of Neural Video Coding (NVC), we implement a hybrid allocation strategy that combines a sliding window for long-term traffic smoothing with dynamic rebalancing within the Group of Pictures (GOP).

As defined in our implementation, the base bit budget for the current GOP is calculated using a sliding window mechanism. This effectively smoothes out short-term fluctuations caused by scene complexity changes. The average target bits per frame, $R_{avg}$, is derived as follows:

\begin{equation}
    R_{avg} = \frac{R_{tgt} \cdot (N_{coded} + SW) - R_{cum}}{SW}
\end{equation}

where $R_{tgt}$ is the user-defined target bitrate, $N_{coded}$ is the number of encoded frames, $R_{cum}$ is the cumulative actual bits consumed, and $SW$ is the sliding window size (typically 40 frames). This formula ensures that if previous frames under-used the budget, the surplus is distributed to future frames, and vice versa.

\subsection{Hierarchical Quality Structure and Feature Propagation}
\subsubsection*{Rationale for Periodic "Neural Anchors"}
In traditional video coding (e.g., H.264), I-frames are inserted periodically to stop error propagation. However, in the DCVC-RT architecture, inserting frequent I-frames is prohibitively expensive in terms of bitrate. Instead, the system relies on the continuous propagation of latent features $F_t$ to serve as temporal contexts for future frames.

This propagation mechanism introduces a unique challenge: \textbf{Feature Degradation}. As the distance from the last refresh point increases, the quality of the propagated feature gradually decays due to accumulated quantization noise and motion alignment errors. If the feature quality drops below a critical threshold, the neural decoder struggles to reconstruct the next frame effectively, leading to a "drifting" visual artifacts.

To mitigate this, we adopt a Hierarchical Quality Structure by applying a periodic weight pattern $\Omega$. We introduce the concept of \textbf{"Neural Anchors"}. By assigning higher weights (and thus \textbf{higher QPs}) to specific frames periodically (e.g., every 4 or 8 frames), we force the encoder to generate high-fidelity reconstructed features. These high-quality features act as anchors, effectively "sharpening" the temporal context. This allows subsequent frames—even those encoded with higher QPs—to reference a cleaner history, thereby improving the overall Rate-Distortion performance of the entire GOP.

\subsection{Dynamic GOP Rebalancing}
To strictly adhere to the GOP budget while respecting the weight pattern, we employ a dynamic rebalancing mechanism. Within a GOP, the target bitrate for the current frame $t$, denoted as $R_{pic}$, is calculated based on the \textit{remaining} GOP budget $R_{rem}$ and the \textit{remaining} weights $\Sigma\omega_{rem}$:

\begin{equation}
    R_{pic} = \max\left(R_{floor}, R_{rem} \cdot \frac{\omega_t}{\sum_{i=t}^{N} \omega_i}\right)
\end{equation}

This dynamic approach ensures that if a high-weight anchor frame consumes less than expected (e.g., due to a static scene), the saved bits are immediately reallocated to the remaining frames in the same GOP, maximizing resource utilization.

\section{Dynamic Smoothing and Control}
\label{sec:control_logic}

\subsection{Smart Output Smoothing (Second-Stage Smoothing)}
After the RLS model predicts the raw quantization parameter ($QP_{raw}$) using the smoothed parameters $\theta_{ema}$, we apply a final safety mechanism known as \textbf{Smart Output Smoothing}.

Directly applying the predicted QP can sometimes lead to visual flickering, especially when the model tries to correct for minor bitrate deviations too aggressively. However, excessive smoothing can lead to buffer overflows during scene cuts. To resolve this trade-off, we propose a dynamic mechanism based on the real-time Error Ratio ($E_t$).

We define the error ratio as the normalized deviation of the actual bitrate from the target over a short window:
\begin{equation}
    E_t = \frac{\text{mean}(E_{window})}{R_{tgt}}
\end{equation}

The final QP is determined by dynamically adjusting the smoothing factor $\gamma$:
\begin{equation}
    QP_{smooth, t} = \gamma \cdot QP_{smooth, t-1} + (1 - \gamma) \cdot QP_{raw, t}
\end{equation}

The logic for $\gamma$ is adaptive:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stability Mode ($E_t < \delta$):} When the controller is tracking well (e.g., error ratio $< 10\%$), we set a high smoothing factor (e.g., $\gamma = 0.8$). This acts as a low-pass filter, ensuring gradual QP transitions and stable visual quality.
    \item \textbf{Emergency Response ($E_t \ge \delta$):} If the error spikes (e.g., due to a sudden scene cut or complex motion), $\gamma$ is set to $0.0$. The controller bypasses the smoother, allowing the RLS prediction to instantly react and bring the bitrate back to the target level.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Algorithm Summary}

The complete proposed method, integrating the sliding window allocation, dynamic GOP rebalancing, and the Double-Smoothing RLS (Parameter EMA + Output Smoothing), is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:proposed}.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
% Standard Academic Algorithm Format
% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{algorithm}[t] 
\caption{Proposed Dual-State RLS with Double-Smoothing Strategy}
\label{alg:proposed} 
\begin{algorithmic}[0] 

    % Input / Output
    \State \textbf{Input:} Target $R_{tgt}$, Weights $\Omega$, Window $SW$, GOP $N$, Margin $\delta$
    \State \textbf{Hyperparams:} Output Smooth $\gamma$, Param EMA $\eta$, RLS Forgetting $\lambda$
    \State \textbf{Output:} Final $QP_t$ for current frame $t$
    
    \vspace{0.5em} 
    \State \textbf{1. System Initialization}
    \State \quad Initialize Dual RLS States: $\hat{\theta}_{Normal}, \hat{\theta}_{Refresh}$, Covariance $\mathbf{P} \leftarrow 100\mathbf{I}$
    \State \quad Initialize Smoothed Params: $\theta_{ema} \leftarrow \hat{\theta}_{Normal}$
    \State \quad Initialize Allocator: $R_{cum} \leftarrow 0$, $N_{coded} \leftarrow 0$, Error Queue $E \leftarrow \emptyset$

    \vspace{0.5em}
    \State \textbf{2. Processing Loop}
    \For{each frame $t$ in video sequence}
    
        \vspace{0.3em}
        \State \textit{// Step A: Sliding-Window \& GOP Budgeting}
        \If{Start of new GOP}
            \State $R_{avg} \leftarrow \max(R_{floor}, [R_{tgt} \cdot (N_{coded} + SW) - R_{cum}] / SW)$
            \State $R_{rem} \leftarrow R_{avg} \cdot N$; \quad $\Sigma\omega_{rem} \leftarrow \sum_{i=1}^{N} \omega_i$
        \EndIf

        \vspace{0.3em}
        \State \textit{// Step B: Bitrate Allocation}
        \State $R_{pic} \leftarrow \max(R_{floor}, R_{rem} \cdot (\omega_t / \Sigma\omega_{rem}))$

        \vspace{0.5em}
        \State \textit{// Step C: Model Prediction (Using Smoothed Parameters)}
        \State Identify Frame Type $T$ (Normal/Refresh) and select active RLS state $\hat{\theta}_{curr}$
        \State Note: Prediction uses $\theta_{ema}$, NOT the instantaneous $\hat{\theta}_{curr}$
        \State $QP_{raw} \leftarrow \theta_{ema}[0] \cdot \ln(R_{pic}) + \theta_{ema}[1]$
        
        \vspace{0.3em}
        \State \textit{// Step D: Smart Output Smoothing}
        \State $e_{ratio} \leftarrow \text{mean}(E) / R_{tgt}$
        \If{$e_{ratio} < \delta$} \quad (\textit{Stability Mode})
            \State $QP_{smooth} \leftarrow \gamma \cdot QP_{t-1} + (1 - \gamma) \cdot QP_{raw}$
        \Else \quad (\textit{Emergency Response})
            \State $QP_{smooth} \leftarrow QP_{raw}$
        \EndIf
        \State $QP_t \leftarrow \text{clip}(\text{round}(QP_{smooth}), QP_{min}, QP_{max})$

        \vspace{0.5em}
        \State \textit{// Step E: State \& Budget Update}
        \State Encode frame $\to$ get $R_{real}$
        \State $R_{cum} \leftarrow R_{cum} + R_{real}$; \quad Update queue $E$
        \State $R_{rem} \leftarrow R_{rem} - R_{real}$; \quad $\Sigma\omega_{rem} \leftarrow \Sigma\omega_{rem} - \omega_t$

        \vspace{0.3em}
        \State \textbf{RLS Update:} Update $\hat{\theta}_{curr}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ using $R_{real}$ and $QP_t$
        \State \textbf{Param Smoothing:} $\theta_{ema} \leftarrow \eta \cdot \theta_{ema} + (1 - \eta) \cdot \hat{\theta}_{curr}$

    \EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
