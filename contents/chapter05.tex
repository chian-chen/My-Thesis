\chapter{Experiments and Results}

\section{Experimental Setup}

To comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed rate control scheme, we conducted extensive experiments on standard video compression benchmarks. This section details the datasets used, the implementation specifics of our framework and baselines, and the evaluation metrics employed.

\subsection{Datasets}

We selected three widely used datasets to evaluate the performance of our method across diverse video contents and motion characteristics. To ensure a fair and consistent comparison, all video sequences were standardized to a resolution of $1920 \times 1080$. Furthermore, we adopted a ``Low-Delay P'' configuration (intra period set to -1), where only the first frame is encoded as an I-frame, and all subsequent frames are encoded as P-frames. We utilized the full length of each video sequence without cropping to test the long-term stability of the rate control algorithm.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{UVG Dataset}~\cite{20}: This dataset offers high frame rate (120fps) sequences in both 4K and 1080p resolutions. For our experiments, we utilized the native 1080p sequences provided by the dataset. The UVG dataset is characterized by high temporal redundancy and smooth motion, making it suitable for evaluating the efficiency of inter-frame prediction and rate stability over long durations. Most sequences in this dataset consist of 600 frames.
    
    \item \textbf{MCL-JCV Dataset}~\cite{26}: The MCL-JCV dataset comprises 30 video sequences with a resolution of $1920 \times 1080$. It covers a wide variety of content types, including distinct textures and motion patterns (e.g., camera panning, zooming, and complex object motion). This diversity is crucial for assessing the generalization capability of the rate control model. The sequence lengths typically range between 120 and 150 frames.
    
    \item \textbf{HEVC Class B Dataset}~\cite{38}: We utilized the standard Class B sequences from the HEVC common test conditions. These sequences are natively $1920 \times 1080$ and are widely used in the literature~\parencite{28, 51} for benchmarking video compression performance. They present challenging scenarios with varying degrees of object motion and background complexity, with lengths ranging from 200 to 600 frames.
\end{itemize}

Since our target scenario is real-time streaming and video conferencing, which typically feature continuous scenes, we applied a specific filtering criterion to the selected datasets. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:hard_cuts}, certain sequences in the HEVC Class B and MCL-JCV datasets contain editorial shot transitions or ``hard cuts.'' These abrupt scene changes can cause instantaneous spikes in bit rate or quality fluctuation, potentially skewing the evaluation of the rate control algorithm's stability in a way that does not reflect our target use case. Therefore, we excluded these sequences to ensure a more relevant assessment. Specifically, the \textit{Kimono} sequence was excluded from the HEVC Class B dataset, and sequences indices [04, 06, 12, 18, 19, 20, 21, 27, 29, 30] were excluded from the MCL-JCV dataset.

\section{Evaluation Benchmarks}

To provide a holistic evaluation, we employed four key metrics focusing on accuracy, quality, stability, and efficiency.

\subsection{Rate Control Accuracy}
The primary goal of rate control is to minimize the deviation between the target bitrate and the actual output bitrate. We measure this using the absolute Bitrate Error (BRE), defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta R = \left| \frac{R_{target} - R_{actual}}{R_{target}} \right| \times 100\%
\end{equation}

\subsection{R-D Performance}
We evaluate the coding efficiency using the Bjøntegaard Delta Rate (BD-Rate)~\cite{06}. BD-Rate measures the percentage of bitrate savings required to achieve the same objective quality compared to an anchor. In our implementation, the BD-Rate is calculated using Piecewise Cubic Hermite Interpolating Polynomial (PCHIP) interpolation in the logarithmic rate domain to ensure curve stability. We utilize two quality metrics for this calculation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PSNR (Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio):} Evaluating pixel-level fidelity.
    \item \textbf{MS-SSIM (Multi-Scale Structural Similarity):} Evaluating perceptual quality.
\end{itemize}

Since raw MS-SSIM values often saturate near 1.0, making direct linear interpolation prone to errors, we transform the MS-SSIM values into the decibel (dB) domain prior to calculating the BD-Rate. This transformation is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    MS\text{-}SSIM_{dB} = -10 \log_{10}(1 - MS\text{-}SSIM)
\end{equation}

This logarithmic scaling aligns the metric's behavior with PSNR, allowing for more consistent R-D curve fitting and comparison.

\subsection{Time Overhead}
We measure the computational complexity using the Time Ratio (TR), defined as the total encoding time of the method with rate control divided by the encoding time of the anchor (without rate control):

\begin{equation}
    TR = \frac{T_{RC}}{T_{Anchor}}
\end{equation}

A TR close to 1.0 indicates negligible computational overhead.

\section{Implementation Details}

\subsection{Experimental Settings}

To ensure a comprehensive evaluation of the rate control algorithms, we configured the DCVC-RT framework with specific encoding parameters (summarized in Fig.~\ref{fig:rd_setup}). 

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Target Rates:} We selected four operating points to represent a wide range of quality levels. The target bitrates ($R_{target}$) were generated using the original DCVC-RT model with base QP values of $\{5, 20, 35, 50\}$.
    \item \textbf{Intra Period:} We adopted a ``Low-Delay P'' configuration by setting the Intra Period to $-1$. This implies that only the very first frame of the sequence is encoded as an I-frame, while all subsequent frames are encoded as P-frames, simulating a real-time streaming scenario.
    \item \textbf{Sequence Length:} Unlike many studies that test only short clips, we utilized the \textbf{full length} of each video sequence (ranging from 100 to 600 frames depending on the dataset) without cropping. This allows us to rigorously test the long-term stability and error propagation of the rate control methods.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Baseline Methods}

We compared our proposed method against several state-of-the-art baselines to validate its performance. The baselines include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anchor (Rule-based):} The rule-based rate control method proposed by the DCVC authors in the Appendix of DCVC-FM~\cite{15}. It incorporates the framework's default inter-frame QP variation patterns to determine the bit budget.
    \item \textbf{Baseline-Lambda (2014)}~\cite{50}: A representative $\lambda$-domain rate control mechanism. Although originally designed for traditional codecs, the underlying $R-\lambda$ relationship remains applicable to modern codecs.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 2022p (Li et al., ICASSP 2022)}~\cite{51}: A pioneering rate control scheme for LVC that proposes an R-D-$\Lambda$ model to characterize inter-frame dependency. It employs a staged parameter update mechanism to ensure robust estimation and accurate rate allocation.
    \item \textbf{Baseline 2024 (Liao et al., ICASSP 2024)}~\cite{52}: A generalizable plug-in rate control scheme that introduces a Rate-Quality ($R-Q_s$) model. Unlike traditional methods, it regulates the bitrate by directly adjusting the scaling parameter $Q_s$ to preserve the hierarchical quality structure of NVC frameworks.
\end{itemize}

For our proposed methods, we evaluate two variations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Proposed-Iter:} Uses the QP-log(R) model with the iterative gradient descent update method.
    \item \textbf{Proposed-RLS:} Utilizes the Recursive Least Squares (RLS) algorithm for parameter updates, which is expected to yield superior stability and adaptability.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Discussion and Insights}

The experimental results across the UVG, HEVC-B, and MCL-JCV datasets reveal a distinct correlation between the temporal stability of the video content and the efficacy of the proposed RLS-based control.

As evidenced by the results, the \textbf{Prop-RLS} method yields the most significant coding efficiency gains on high-frame-rate datasets with stable motion. On the UVG dataset (120 fps), which features smooth camera movements and high temporal redundancy, our method achieves a substantial BD-Rate reduction of \textbf{14.9\%}. Similarly, on the HEVC Class B dataset (typically 50--60 fps), sequences like \textit{BasketballDrive} and \textit{Cactus} exhibit strong performance improvements. This can be attributed to the RLS algorithm's ability to effectively leverage historical data to refine its parameter estimation when inter-frame correlations are strong and consistent.

Conversely, for content with lower frame rates or more erratic motion, the advantage of the RLS approach becomes less pronounced. For instance, in the \textit{ParkScene} sequence (24 fps), while the RLS method still outperforms the Baseline-Lambda in terms of BD-Rate (-24.3\% vs. -22.7\%), the margin is narrower compared to the 60 fps sequences, and the bitrate error (BRE) slightly increases. In such complex, fast-changing scenarios, the historical data becomes less predictive of future frames, causing the performance to converge towards that of a standard gradient-based controller.

In conclusion, our proposed method aligns well with the target scenario of \textbf{video conferencing and real-time streaming}, which typically involves fixed-camera setups, stable viewpoints, and continuous scenes. Under these conditions, the RLS-based rate control successfully maximizes coding efficiency while maintaining stable bitrate regulation.

\section{Parametric Analysis and Robustness}

A key advantage of the proposed RLS-based controller is its robustness against hyperparameter initialization and tuning. Traditional gradient-based methods are typically sensitive to initial values and step sizes (learning rates), often requiring precise tuning to avoid divergence. To validate the superiority of our method, we conducted extensive ablation studies focusing on parameter initialization and update dynamics.

\section{Summary}

In this chapter, we presented a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed rate control framework within the DCVC-RT architecture. Through extensive experiments across the UVG, MCL-JCV, and HEVC Class B datasets, we validated the superiority of the RLS-based approach compared to both rule-based and gradient-based baselines.

The key findings from our experiments can be summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Superior Coding Efficiency:} The Proposed-RLS method consistently achieved the best Rate-Distortion performance, yielding an average BD-Rate reduction of 14.9\% on the UVG dataset. The insights reveal that the method is particularly effective for high-frame-rate sequences and continuous scenes typical of video conferencing.
    \item \textbf{High Accuracy and Stability:} With an average Bitrate Error (BRE) of 1.92\%, the proposed controller demonstrated precise rate regulation capabilities, effectively minimizing deviations from the target bandwidth.
    \item \textbf{Robustness:} Ablation studies confirmed that the RLS algorithm exhibits remarkable robustness against initial parameter mismatches and is insensitive to hyperparameter variations. This contrasts sharply with traditional gradient-based methods, which are prone to divergence under suboptimal settings.
    \item \textbf{Real-Time Feasibility:} The computational complexity analysis showed that our method introduces negligible overhead (Time Ratio $\approx$ 1.01), ensuring that the real-time performance of DCVC-RT is preserved.
\end{itemize}

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the proposed RLS-based rate control scheme provides a robust, efficient, and high-performance solution tailored for real-time neural video coding applications.
